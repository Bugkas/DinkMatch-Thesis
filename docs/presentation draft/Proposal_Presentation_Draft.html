The Problem: Walk-In Racket Clubs

  • What is the problem? Recreational clubs use manual "paddle racks" (First-In, First-Out). Players physically stack their rackets to line up for a court.
  • Why does it exist? Clubs lack affordable digital systems to organize "walk-in" players in real-time.
  • How does this affect the players?
    • Unfair Matches: Beginners face experts just because they lined up at the same time.
    • Cheating the Line (Ghost Stacking): Players skip turns or hide paddles to avoid playing with strangers.
    • Empty Courts: Courts stay empty during peak hours because human organizers get overwhelmed tracking who is next.
Slide 2
Presenter 1

The Research Gap

  • Software like CourtReserve exists, but it's only for booking a court days in advance.
  • Modern video games match players perfectly by skill, but they require massive, expensive cloud servers and continuous internet connections.
  • The Gap: There is no digital system built for physical racket clubs that automatically organizes walk-in players by skill in real-time, using low-cost hardware that works even if the internet goes down.

Our Proposed Solution: DinkMatch

  • We are replacing the physical paddle rack with a Digital Touchscreen Kiosk.
  • Players simply type a 4-digit PIN to check-in. The system acts as a "Virtual Organizer", automatically grouping players into fair matches based on their hidden skill ratings.
  • Preview of our 3 Objectives:
    • Objective 1: Design the math and rules for skill rating.
    • Objective 2: Build the software kiosk and AI Referee.
    • Objective 3: Test the system with simulations and real players.

Methodology: Mixed Research Design

Why mixed? Because we must first build the system, and then test it scientifically.
  • 1. Developmental Phase (Building it):
    • This phase is where we design the mathematical formulas, write the code, and build the touchscreen kiosk software (following the Software Development Life Cycle).
  • 2. Experimental Testing Phase (Proving it works):
    • This phase is where we put the finished software in a controlled environment to test it. We will test it using computer simulations (fake players) and field trials (real players) to prove it solves the problem.

Presenter 3

Specific Objective 1: The Rating System

Objective 1: Design and adapt a stateless PM-Elo rating engine and a multi-objective matchmaking solver.
  • What this means: We are designing the mathematical "brain" of the system.
  • The Matchmaking Logic: The algorithm constantly balances two goals: matching players of identical skill levels, while making sure nobody waits in line too long.
Slide 7
Presenter 3

SOO 1: Data Gathered (System Parameters)

The strict rules we program into the math formula:
  • Starting Rating: Every new player starts at the exact same baseline (e.g., 1200 points).
  • Scaling Factor (K-Factor): A strict limit on how much a player's score can change in one single game. This prevents a player from reaching "Pro" rank overnight just by getting lucky.
  • Anti-Carry Discount: A rule that prevents a highly-skilled player from artificially boosting a beginner friend's rating during a doubles match.
Slide 8
Presenter 3

SOO 1: Data Analysis (Mathematical Verification)

  • How do we prove the math works? We test the formula to guarantee the "Zero-Sum" rule is never broken.
  • What is Zero-Sum? Think of points as a currency. The exact amount of points gained by the winning team is subtracted from the losing team.
  • By verifying this mathematically, we ensure that points are never "magically created," meaning the player ratings will never artificially spiral out of control over time.

Specific Objective 2: Building the App

Objective 2: Develop the DinkMatch system featuring an offline-first kiosk, cloud sync, and an edge AI line-fault detector.
  • The Kiosk: We will build the touchscreen app. It is "Offline-First," meaning if the club loses internet, the queue keeps running perfectly locally.
  • The AI Referee (Computer Vision): We will point a basic webcam at the court line. An AI tracks the players' feet and sounds an alarm if they step on the line (foot-fault).

Specific Objective 3.1: Computer Simulations

Objective 3.1: Evaluate matchmaking efficiency and queue dynamics using Monte Carlo simulations.
  • What is a Monte Carlo Simulation? It is a computer program that generates thousands of "fake" virtual players arriving at a court to test our queue logic.
  • Why do we do this? Instead of waiting weeks to test rush-hour traffic in real life, we can simulate 50 players flooding the court in seconds. This stress-tests the system before humans use it.

Presenter 5

SOO 3.1: Data Gathered & Data Analysis

  • Data Gathered:
    • How long did the virtual players wait in line? (Queue Wait Time)
    • How far apart in skill were the players matched together? (Skill Variance)
  • Data Analysis:
    • We will use statistical tests (T-tests and ANOVA) to compare DinkMatch against the old manual paddle rack.
    • This will mathematically prove whether our digital system is faster and fairer than the manual physical line.

Specific Objective 3.2: Real-World Testing

Objective 3.2: Evaluate rating calibration accuracy compared to expert manual rankings.
  • What this means: We need to prove that the AI actually knows who is good and who is a beginner.
  • The Test: We will bring the completed system to a local club and have 20 real players use it for a live session.

Presenter 5

SOO 3.2: Data Gathered & Data Analysis

  • Data Gathered:
    • Before they play, a human Coach will rank the 20 players based on real-life experience.
    • After the session, we will extract the automated rankings generated by our AI.
  • Data Analysis:
    • We will use Spearman’s Rank Correlation.
    • This statistical tool compares the AI's list against the Coach's list. If they match closely, we prove the AI successfully evaluated player skill without human help.
